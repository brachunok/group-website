What do we need to understand about a community before trying to represent it through a model? This question became more meaningful during my participation in the intensive academy of the KIETS Climate Leaders Program. For one week in May 2026, we visited Down East in Carteret County, North Carolina. Through conversations with local residents, we explored the connections between economic activities, especially tourism, the natural environment, and vulnerability to flooding.

These conversations helped me see how closely the benefits and challenges of tourism development are connected. Tourism creates business opportunities and supports family incomes, while its growth also raises concerns about housing costs, pressure on basic services, and ecosystem conservation. These ecosystems support local livelihoods and are part of what makes the region attractive. Protecting them and benefiting from them economically can therefore support each other, but can also create tensions.

What stood out to me was that these tensions can exist within the same person. Someone may depend on tourism while also worrying about its effects on the region. As someone joked, “We like it when visitors come, but we like it even more when they go home.” These differences also go beyond opinions: they involve experiences, available resources, and values about what should come first.

As a researcher interested in building models to support decisions about climate risks, this experience made me rethink my expectations. Including differences between stakeholders can help us explore how the same decision affects people in different ways. However, representing all their perspectives is neither realistic nor necessary for a model to be useful. The challenge is to identify which interactions matter for the decision we want to study.

For example, understanding individual decisions also requires considering the economic constraints, infrastructure, and institutions that shape the available options. From the lab, it is easy to explain a choice as a preference when it actually reflects a lack of options.

Above all, I came away with a greater sense of humility about what modeling can achieve. Listening to different stakeholders can help us question assumptions, identify missing mechanisms, and choose which outcomes are worth evaluating. Although these conversations cannot capture every perspective or validate a model on their own, they can help us ask better questions and recognize when we need insights from other disciplines.

Since every model requires simplification, a better understanding of the system’s complexity helps us recognize what we are leaving out. The conversations in Down East reminded me that this understanding begins with listening to people describe their experiences and the options they see as available to them.

Having the opportunity to take part in these activities enriched my doctoral training. It also raised new questions as I compared what I heard with rural dynamics I have seen in my own country. In my future work, I want to check my modeling assumptions against the knowledge and experiences of the people those models represent.
